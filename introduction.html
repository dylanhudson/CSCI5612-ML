Introduction

Topic: Cancer Causes and Risk Factors

Cancer is a major cause of death, disability, and immense suffering globally. According to the American Cancer Society in Siegel et al., "2,114,850 new cancer cases and 626,140 cancer deaths are projected to occur in the United States." Of course, these statistics do not capture the extreme suffering experienced by patients (and families/friends), and the economic cost to society in lost productivity and treatment cost. While the direct etiology is known to be genetic mutations that allow uncontrolled and pathologic cell growth, the causes of the particular mutations that result in disease are complex and multifaceted. A wide variety of factors are know to contribute to risk, including environmental expsoures to chemicals, radiation, and certain viral agents, as well as lifestyle and health factors like tobacco use, obesity, and more. Genetic predisposition also plays a role, with certain inherited mutations conferring increased risk for specific cancers, and certain genetic and proteomic profiles being associated with disease prognosis and interaction with other risk factors. Further complicating the causal scenario are the interactions of all the factors, and complex socioeconomic forces that can correlate and influence the more proximate causal factors. Understanding the web of risk is integral for nearly any aspect of research progress, whether public health and policy, scientific progress and frontier advancement in genetic and personalized medicine, and socioeconomic justice and health equity. As both science and technology advance, the amount of available data across all these domains is increasing. Techniques to analyze it and the computing power necessary to do so are advancing as well, and expanding our oppotunties to make discoveries in new ways that were not previously possible.

Certain aspects of this enormous problem of understanding cancer etiology and risk may lend themselves to machine learning techniques, especially as more and more data is generated and made available. According to the Cancer Research Institute, ML methods can bring "immeasurable efficiency when it comes to aggregating, recalling, and contextualizing complicated datasets without the element of human error." Cleaning and analyzing datasets across many different domains is no small task, but may hold promise to identify novel correlations, patterns, or associations that may indicate direction for clinical treatment or public health work and policy. Technology is progressing quickly, but many of these techniques are still in their infancy, as researchers train to develop expertise in 'big data' methods and apply them to cancer biology and medicine. Emerging model evaluations on exposure and risk factor show promising results, as in a recent study by Ahmed et al. which reports 'Categorical Boosting' models outperformed "both traditional and other advanced models." Fully operationalizing this data ultimately in the service of patients will be a Herculean task, not least because of the barriers to data access, the sheer amount of data, and the need for so many disparate types of often sensitive and protected health information. If other media types are included, such as radiological and histological images, the complexity and data requirements only increase. Genomics and proteomics data are of particular interest, especially in the the fields of "multi-omics" and "integrative omics", which work to integrate data across multiple domains to identify complex interactions and patterns that may not be apparent when analyzing data from a limited domain, or when limited by the traditional boundaries of academic fields. The datasets are often very high-dimensional, which also makes them suited for ML modeling, but also presents challenges in the demand for compute power, and the need for dimensionality reduction and feature selection techniques. While vast amounts of multimodal data can be extremely difficult to understand from a granular level, ML techniques may help researchers develop a systems-level understanding of all these complex interrelations.

Machine learning may have multiple roles to play in the future of cancer research and therapeutics development. One application is the creation of diagnostic and clinical tools that can analyze complex and multimodel data, with the goals of catching cancer earlier, and characterizing it more accurately to target therapy as rapidly as possible. Another is predictive risk-modeling, which may guide prevention strategies and policy. A less direct application is the further discovery of relevant biological mechanisms and processes. For example, if the conditions of tumorigenesis and metastasis can be better understood, this may lead to the development of novel therapeutics that can target those processes. Unfortuntalye, these pathological processes involve complex and multi-level, multi-system interaction that are usually part of healthy physiology. This means that particularly subtle changes must be noted to recognize pathology, and that most therapies will also inadvertently target healthy tissue- a significant source of side effects and further suffering for patients. In addition to all these applications for computing, collaboration with experts in other fields will be crucial. For example, public health and policy experts will be needed to translate research findings into effective and equitable policy, and to understand the complex socioeconomic factors that influence risk and outcomes. Clinical specialists will be needed to understand ways that findings might translate into procedures. Interdisciplinary knowledge and skillsets will be needed to bridge the wide gaps between deparments and fields, and to turn results into action.

While many opportunties exist for new technological methods to drive discovery and progress, we face many challenges as well. Even though more data is being collected and generated than ever before, access and formatting create a labyrinthine ordeal to navigate for researchers and scientists. With the rate of technological change, there may be methods and techniques that are not even conceived of yet, much less developed and implemented. While this may be the "Age of Information", there is much to be done on determining how to collect, clean, and use all the information. Fortunately, methods and computing power are advancing rapidly. Cost remains a barrier for many researchers and institutions, as high-performance compute is extremely expensive, whether managed on-prem or purchased from a cloud provider. Still, personal hardware is increasing in performance, especially vis-a-vis GPU capabilities, which may help individuals and teams run advanced analyses and deal with big data while avoiding some of the legacy barriers to HPC system access. As capabilities increase, so does the rate of advancement, so predicting what will be possible in ever the near future gets more difficult. Additionally, when major breakthoughs occur, resources are more likely to be mobilized to support them, which can further increase the pace of development. Despite the recent deep cuts to science and technology funding, hopefully support will continue in the long-term to help achieve breakthoughs in science, medicine, and climate change challenges.

Questions of Interest:

  • Do environmental risk factors like air pollution and pesticide use associate with cancer incidence?
  • Can combinations of socio-economic and lifestyle factors predict cancer risk?
  • Can survival rates or other prognostic outcomes be predicted from genomic, clinicial, or proteomic data?
  • What are the most important risk factors for different types of cancer, and how do they interact?
  • Are there distinct clusters of patients with similar risk profiles or prognoses?
  • Can associations between risk factors and genetic profiles be identified that may suggest complex biological interactions?
  • Are other comorbid health conditions associated with increased cancer risk or worse outcomes?
  • Are there geographic patterns or clusters that suggest unknown factors that may be associated with certain areas?
  • Can we predict cancer incidence from county-level exposure and health data?
  • Can we relate genetic and protein expression data to environmental exposures and socioeconomic factors to identify potential interactions?
