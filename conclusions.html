Conclusions

Public health and cancer research are each enormously complex challenges in their own rights. Analyzing aspects of both together is perhaps even more challenging. The signals can be subtle, complex, noisy, and confounding factors are omnipresent, presenting a particularly onerous statistical challenge, to say nothing of the medical/clinical issues. As in most data analysis problems, obtaining quality data is often the first and most difficult obstacle to overcome, if it can be at all. Only with quality data can meaningful analysis and modeling be performed to extract useful signals from the noise. Due to the high amount of effort required to collect and prepare useful data (biopsies, lab work, clinical work, etc), and the need for privacy and control of such sensitive information as health data, accumulating sufficient data, especially in the quantities required for machine learning, is difficult even for professional researchers at institutions. Much of the data that would be useful to train diagnostic tools or meaningfully predictive public health models is not readily available, as it either involves personal information, would require large samples of healthy tissues or genomes/proteomes as controls, which is not usually provided in disease-related datasets, if it is even available at the scale necessary for effective use. Consequently, the range of questions that can be posed and answered by lay-people and within the scope of such projects as this is limited. However, it does provide an opportunity to validate certain modeling techniques, develop data cleaning and preparation pipelines, and build interesting models that can suggest directions for further learning and research.

In this project, various data cleaning, EDA, clustering, dimensionality reduction, and machine learning techniques were applied to the rather patchy assortment of datasets obtainable within the realm of publically available health and patient-level cancer data. Even these basic applications on the limited data showed that signal could be extracted, and that many of these techniques could be extremely useful when applied to larger datasets, and with more domain-motivated questions. For example, the proteomics data from the Proteomic Data Commons provides data on thousands of different proteins in various cancerous tissue types. While it is fairly straightforward to run PCA or clustering on this data, understanding the relevant biology is no small task. Proteins interact in complex ways, with many celluar processes involving many proteins acting together, and many systems-level interactions that can change in various ways if disease causes imbalance. One takeaway from this multi-faceted data analysis is the importance of domain knowledge in developing useful questions, as understanding the context and the underlying biological, medical, and public health principles is crucial for formulating meaningful analyses and interpreting results correctly- or perhaps even more important, avoid misinterpretation or drawing incorrect conclusions from the data. In this vein, working with these disparate datasets shows the need for interdisciplinary knowledge, and 'specialized generalists' who can bridge unrelated domains, link insights across fields, and identify opportunities for discovery. Building connections to clinical domains is also critical; fascinating research or statistical questions do not always translate to actionable interventions or therapes. Of course not all research sure be driven by immediate applicability, but rather the more information that can flow between domains, the better the needs of both can be met, and the more impactful the research and analysis can be for both public health and clinical practice.

Despite the data sourcings issues and limitations, there were some interesting findings. One of the most intriguing is the range of socioeconomic and geographic cancer incidence clusters identified in the clustering analysis performed in this project. Some groupings were expected, where low education and high unemployment formed clusters with high cancer incidence, but most were more subtle, and had clear georaphic clustering when visualized on a map, with the clusters corresponding to contiguous regions. Instead of clear monotonic associations with socioeconomic variables, clustering reveals how even just a few features can be interelated, and act as proxies for other underlying factors. The clusters showing up as geographic regions suggests many avenues of inquiry for causal mechanisms or other related factors, from environmental exposures, to distance to closest health centers, to regional/cultural attidudes, to legal or policy differences, etc. This also underscores the value of unsupervised methods in exploring datasets prior to modeling; seeing that there were geographic patterns motivated the use of spatially aware models later (the neural networks section). Understanding these types of patterns is an area of recent and active research in clinical care, public health, and health equity, and can inform major decisions, such as resource allocation, policy development, and targeted interventions to improve health outcomes where they are most needed. Historically, certain populations are underserved and suffer disproportionately, and understanding the geographic and socioeconomic patterns of disease incidence can help guide interventions to address these disparities. However, caution must be exercised; as Ruha Benjamin writes in "Race after Technology", data-driven methods can also encode existing systemic bias, leading to false conclusions or perpetuating the problems (or simply failing to recognize them).

Realistically, this project was likely over-ambitious in seeking to investigate the original questions posed in the introduction. Putting all the project sections together primarily serves to illustrate the scope of these modelling challenges. Each techniques provides a slightly different perspective on the data, and each dataset provides a different perspective on the tangled web of interacting factors that influence cancer rates and disease progression. As a learning tool, this project certainly provides an important and interesting topic with nearly infinite depth to explore the modelling process and motivate further investigation into public health and biology. However, it is also exciting that meaningful signal can be detected even in such small and incomplete datasets. For example, it is perhaps unfortunate that the per-type cancer rate used in the neural networks section had suppressed data where absolute rates were lowest (below 16 cases). The geographic and socioeconomic clustering suggested that these rural areas are possibly the most at-risk both for those factors and environmental exposures, but despite this lacuna, the work of hypothesis generation is still accomplished: The data may not show anything itself, but instead it points where to look. Certainly much more data and validation would needed to draw real conclusions, but the process of developing a hypothesis is extremely important, especially in complex and cross-disciplinary fields. As research data availability increases, it is ever more important to efficiently separate signal from noise. Resources are far too scarce, both financially and intellectually, to waste, especially when working on solving humanity's biggest challenges.

The challenges of cancer research and therapy development may be immense, but the outlook is bright (despite recent funding cuts slowing progress). Researchers are extremely active, building databases and sharing information to increase breakthroughs globally. Extensive use of data science and statistical methods is currently advancing some of the most immediately pressing concerns around cancer today. One topic receiving recent news coverage is the highly concerning doubling of colorectal cancer rates in individuals under 50, for which no cause was identifiable. By mid-2025, genomics researchers were able to uncover signs of early-childhood exposure to particular types of E. coli bacteria that produced a DNA-damaging toxin by analyzing genome sequences and controlling for other factors . This is an excellent example of how statiscal methods can be leveraged to uncover causal hypotheses in emerging cancer incidence trends in the face of urgent need. It is important also, in the face of rapid advancement in data-driven science and technology, to resist the urge to use machine learning methods on every problem. Certainly they are not a panacea for any scientific barrier, and used injudiciously, can be a resource-sink and lead to dead-ends or incorrect conclusions. Data never tells the entire story, and expertise, critical thinking, and quality assurance are all needed to produce good results and avoid harm. Sometimes, simpler techniques may be more appropriate or just as effective. As with any type of technology, it can be used for good or bad, and reflective, careful thinking is paramount in their effective use.
